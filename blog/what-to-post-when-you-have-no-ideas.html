What to post when you genuinely have no ideas

It is Wednesday. You posted on Monday. You know you should post again. You open the app, look at the empty caption box, and close it. This is not a discipline problem, and the fix is not trying harder.

Why the ideas run out

People assume consistency is about motivation. It is not. It is about how many decisions you have to make before you can start.

Every time you sit down to post, you decide what to talk about, which platform, which format, how to open, and whether it is any good. Five decisions before a single word. Do that three times a week and you are making fifteen small judgement calls that drain you before the work begins.

Monday you have energy, so you post. Wednesday you are tired, so you skip. By Friday you have decided to start properly next month.

The fix is not more inspiration. It is fewer decisions.

Seven places a post is already hiding

You do not need a new idea. You need to look where the ideas already are.

1. Your sent messages

Scroll your WhatsApp or DMs from the last week. Every time you explained something to a customer, that explanation is a post. You have already written it and you already know it is a real question, because someone actually asked.

2. The thing that annoyed you this week

Something in your industry irritated you. A bad practice, a myth, a competitor overcharging. Annoyance is the easiest thing to write about because you do not have to manufacture an opinion.

3. A mistake you made

Not a fake humblebrag mistake. A real one, and what you changed afterwards. These perform well because almost nobody posts them.

4. Something you learned the hard way

What took you two years to understand that you could explain in ninety seconds. That gap between what you know and what your customer knows is where all your best content lives.

5. Your own old posts

Take the one that did best six months ago and say it again, differently. Most of your audience never saw it. The ones who did have forgotten. Repeating what works is a strategy, not laziness.

6. A question you would ask a competitor

If you could ask the best person in your field one thing, what would it be. Post the question. Then post your own attempt at the answer.

7. What you are working on right now

Not the finished thing. The half-finished thing. Process is more interesting than results, and you have process available every single day.

Set a ten minute timer. Pick one of the seven, write badly, post it. A mediocre post that exists beats a brilliant one you never wrote. You can tell which posts people actually remember, and it is almost never the polished ones.

Stop waiting to feel ready

Inspiration is not a strategy, because it does not arrive on schedule. Everyone who posts consistently has a system, and the system is usually boring.

Decide once, in advance, what each day is for. Monday is a question you get asked. Wednesday is proof your thing works. Friday is a person. Now Wednesday is not "what should I post," it is "which proof." One decision instead of five.

The deeper fix: decide in batches

The reason Wednesday is hard is that you are deciding on Wednesday. Deciding is the expensive part, not writing.

Sit down once a week, or once a month, and list the ideas. Do not write the posts. Just list what each one is about. Then on Wednesday you are not facing a blank page, you are facing a note that says "answer the delivery question." That is a completely different task and it takes a fraction of the energy.

The people who post consistently are not more creative than you. They just decided earlier.

When it is actually burnout

One honest caveat. Sometimes having no ideas is not a systems problem. If you have been forcing content for months and feel nothing but dread, that is not fixed by a better process.

Take a week off properly. Announce it if you like, or do not. The algorithm will forgive you faster than you think, and coming back with something you actually want to say is worth more than four weeks of posting on empty.
